Landscaping options and costs in Burlington

Burlington homeowners have a lot of landscaping opportunity because most residential areas are set up for a real yard—about 50.3% of Ontario dwellings are single-detached, and that footprint is common enough here to support meaningful front-and-back upgrades. Ownership also matters: in Burlington’s wider regional profile, 75.1% of households own (Statistics Canada, 2021 Census), so many projects are planned for the long term rather than “quick curb appeal.” With a median household income around $110,000 (Statistics Canada, 2021 Census), you’ll typically see budgets that can support proper grading, quality base, and durable materials, especially on patios and retaining walls.

In the Hamilton–Niagara Peninsula (hardiness zones 6b–7a), mild winters by Canadian standards extend the workable installation window roughly from late April/early May to mid–October. That helps contractor scheduling and reduces costly delays, but it doesn’t eliminate weather risk—spring freeze–thaw and occasional saturated conditions can affect excavation pricing and base prep. Demand is especially strong in family-oriented areas like Aldershot and Bayview/Appleby corridors, where older homes built before 1981 (47.4% of dwellings in the region) often need drainage corrections before new planting or hardscape goes in. Those factors are why “similar-looking” yards can land in very different ranges across Burlington, and why comparing options line-by-line is the fastest way to understand value—let’s start with the common package types below.

What affects the price of landscaping in Burlington

In Burlington, quotes for what looks like the “same job” can swing by 30–50% across the Hamilton–Niagara Peninsula because the cost drivers aren’t visible from the curb. A lot of pricing comes down to how much excavation and base work is required, whether drainage is being corrected, and how much hand labour it takes to deliver materials to the right locations. Even in our zones 6b–7a climate, the workable window (about late April/early May through mid–October) can tighten scheduling, and that affects labour availability and the time crews can spend on careful prep.

Hardiness zone and frost dates shape both planting success and hardscape engineering. Freeze–thaw and winter wind mean patios and retaining walls need base prep that performs through expansion and contraction, so contractors may need deeper base and better drainage to prevent heaving. In prairie zones (2–4), retaining wall base depth and drought-tolerant plant palettes are even more demanding; in coastal BC (zone 7–8), builders often get a longer softscape season and lush growth, but they still must solve drainage well in heavy-rainfall areas. For Southern Ontario (zone 5–6, including Burlington’s practical range), we balance a moderate season with summer dry spells, so irrigation design is often “recommended” for establishment even if strict rationing isn’t typical.

Concrete Burlington examples: (1) If your property has a sloped front walk and water runs toward the foundation, grading/drainage adjustments can push a patio project from the mid band into higher numbers (and that pairs with the $40–$150 per-sq-ft style hardscape pricing). (2) Upgrading from seeding to sod can multiply lawn costs—often closer to the higher end of the $2–$7 per sq ft sod band versus seeding—only worth it when you need instant coverage (pets, erosion-prone slopes, or early-season usability). (3) Many older homes built before 1981 still have older soil conditions; poor topsoil thickness or compaction means more soil amendment and labour, which compounds quickly.

Permits & regulations in Ontario

In Ontario, most typical landscaping work—such as planting beds, sodding, lawn seeding, mulch installation, and installing softscape features—usually does not require a permit. However, retaining walls are the common exception. In many Ontario municipalities, retaining walls above a height threshold (often around 1.0 m / 3.3 ft) typically require a building permit and sometimes engineered drawings, because the structure must be designed to resist soil pressure and water forces behind the wall. If your project includes changes to lot grading that redirect drainage toward a neighbouring property or connect flows improperly to storm services, additional approval may be triggered as well.

Before any digging for planting holes or wall footings, always plan for utility locates (Ontario “Call Before You Dig” through the provincial locating system). That step protects buried services and prevents costly delays. For homeowners, also confirm local irrigation and water-use rules where applicable—even though the Hamilton–Niagara Peninsula doesn’t face the same intense restrictions as some prairie or dry interior regions, Ontario municipalities can still enforce watering limits during dry periods.

Step-by-step, here’s how to verify a contractor for a Burlington project: (1) Ask for their Ontario business licensing details (where applicable in your area) and confirm they’re operating as a legitimate business. (2) Request a Certificate of Insurance showing general liability (and confirm limits). (3) Verify WSIB/WCB coverage (or your contractor’s applicable clearance/coverage document). (4) Match the contractor’s name/company on the certificate to the contract you sign, and keep copies with your job paperwork.

Planning a landscape that thrives in Burlington's climate

The best Burlington landscapes work with our zone 6b–7a conditions and the local freeze–thaw reality—rather than trying to “force” plants or bases to perform without proper drainage. Start with plant selection: choose species that reliably overwinter here, including native and zone-appropriate shrubs and perennials that don’t demand constant winter protection. For lawns, decide early between sod and seed based on how fast you need results. Sod gives instant coverage, but it’s typically about 3–4x the per-sq-ft cost of seeding once labour and prep are included; it’s also less forgiving if base grading or drainage is wrong.

Drainage and grading are critical across the Hamilton–Niagara Peninsula because saturated spring soils combined with later freezes can create pooling and foundation moisture. For patios and retaining walls, base preparation must account for local frost depth performance: the compacted base layer and drainage pathway behind blocks help prevent heaving and movement. If you’re adding an interlocking patio, don’t treat it like surface-level work—proper excavation, geotextile separation, compacted aggregate, and correct slope matter as much as the pavers.

For low maintenance, use mulch, groundcovers, and well-spaced perennials to reduce weeding and annual upkeep while keeping curb appeal strong. If you want a low-water approach, build in rain-garden pockets and selective irrigation zones rather than trying to remove all water-loving needs. Example: if you’re replacing a tired lawn strip with a sod area costing in the $2–$7 per sq ft range and you only need “instant green” for a short summer window, seeding can be the smarter spend. But if erosion control is urgent on a slope or you need a usable yard for pets within weeks, paying for sod prep and installation can be justified.

How to choose a landscaping contractor in Burlington

Choosing the right landscaping contractor in Burlington starts with verification and clear documentation. First, licensing and coverage: in Ontario, ask for proof of general liability insurance and confirm WSIB/WCB coverage (or the contractor’s applicable clearance/coverage document). You can also ask for their WSIB/WCB clearance reference and confirm dates match your project timeframe. Second, request proof of coverage certificates that name the correct legal business and include coverage limits.

Then get 2–3 itemised, written quotes. You want a breakdown that separates labour and materials (topsoil/aggregate, pavers, blocks, sod, compost, edging, drainage components, disposal). Avoid “lump sum only” estimates unless the scope is exceptionally detailed. Read the scope for exclusions: who hauls away spoil, who handles site clean-up, whether disposal is included, whether utility locates are your responsibility, and whether any permits are included. For retaining walls that may require permitting above common thresholds (often around 1.0 m), clarity matters.

Warranty is another differentiator. Ask for (1) workmanship warranty length and what it covers, (2) product/manufacturer warranties on pavers/blocks/sod where applicable, and (3) whether warranties transfer if you sell the home. For payments, never pay more than 10–15% upfront; use a holdback tied to completion and final cleanup. Finally, insist on a written timeline: a start date, estimated duration, and a completion target—especially because Burlington projects often align with the late April/early May to mid–October work window.

Frequently asked questions — landscaping in Burlington

How do I design a low-water yard in Burlington?

In Burlington, low-water design usually means focusing on planting choices and how you manage establishment, not eliminating water entirely. Burlington sits in a mild-but-variable zone (6b–7a), with summer dry spells, so it helps to use native perennial gardens and mulched shrub borders that retain moisture and overwinter reliably. Start by improving soil structure (compost/topsoil where needed) and add mulch to reduce evaporation. For harder-to-tame areas, consider a rain-garden pocket to capture runoff rather than sending it straight to the street. If you need irrigation, design it with drip zones so established plants get targeted moisture only when needed. As a budget anchor, many homeowners spend within typical landscaping design & installation bands (from $5,000 – $50,000) when drainage improvements and a planting plan are included.

How do I find a licensed landscaper in Burlington?

To find a reliable landscaper in Burlington, start by verifying coverage and legitimacy—not just credentials on a website. Ask for proof of general liability insurance (Certificate of Insurance) and confirm WSIB/WCB coverage is active for the dates of your project; request the clearance documentation if applicable. Then check the actual company name on the certificates matches the contractor on your contract. Next, request 2–3 written, itemised quotes with labour and materials broken out, so you can compare apples to apples. Finally, confirm the contractor’s approach to Ontario requirements for your scope: most planting/sodding typically doesn’t need a permit, but retaining walls above common thresholds (often around 1.0 m) may require permitting. If a contractor refuses to provide insurance/coverage proof or won’t itemise drainage and base work, treat that as a serious concern.

How much does landscaping cost in Burlington?

Landscaping in Burlington typically spans a wide range because scope changes fast—especially when drainage corrections, excavation, and hardscape are involved. For example, full landscape design and installation commonly falls in the broad band of $5,000 – $50,000 depending on whether you’re adding patios, retaining walls, irrigation, and a full planting program. If you’re focused on lawn only, sod installation generally prices per square foot and lands around the $2,XXX–$7 band; seeding can be notably lower when existing soil conditions are acceptable. Interlocking patios and walkways often price by square footage in the $40–$150 range due to base prep and paver installation. A realistic budget is to expect site grading and drainage to matter as much as the visible finishes, particularly for older homes where soils and slopes may not drain cleanly.

What plants thrive in Burlington's climate zone?

Burlington’s practical planting window and overwintering performance are driven by our local zone conditions (roughly 6b–7a) and freeze–thaw winters. Plants that thrive here are usually those that are hardy, not overly tender, and suited to Ontario’s snow loads and seasonal temperature swings. In general, you’ll do well with native or zone-appropriate shrubs and perennials (mulched shrub borders and mixed native perennial gardens are especially dependable). For trees, deciduous species that tolerate winter wind and spring thaw are commonly used. The key is matching plants to your microclimate: sun exposure, drainage, and soil type vary across a lot. If you’re building a low-water yard, pair drought-tolerant natives with proper mulch and (if needed) drip irrigation for establishment. A planting plan bundled into tree and garden planting budgets—often within $50–$1,500 per plant or installation unit depending on size—helps avoid buying the wrong sizes for the job.

Sod vs seed — which is better for a Burlington lawn?

Sod and seed both work in Burlington, but they solve different problems. Sod is best when you need instant coverage—like erosion-prone areas, pet-friendly usable lawn quickly, or when you must stop turf loss immediately. The downside is cost: sod installation commonly lands in the $2–$7 per square foot range once you include grading and proper laying/rolling. Seeding is usually more budget-friendly and can be very successful if your base is graded correctly and you manage moisture during germination. In Burlington, timing matters: early spring and early fall tend to be better for seed success, while sod is commonly installed in late spring (and sometimes early September) so it can root before winter. Whichever you choose, the biggest factor is how your soil drains after rain and spring melt—if drainage is off, neither sod nor seed will perform well.

How much does a retaining wall cost in Burlington?

Retaining wall cost in Burlington depends on height, length, soil conditions, and whether a proper drainage system is included behind the wall. Interlocking block walls are engineered in practice with compacted base and drainage layers (weeping stone/filter fabric), because freeze–thaw can push water and pressure through a poorly built wall. For many typical residential projects, retaining walls fall into a wide band; a common estimate range for an interlocking-block retaining wall project is $8,000–$25,000. If your design needs significant grading changes, a deeper base, or site access constraints (tight side yards, long carry distances), costs increase. Also confirm permitting: retaining walls above a common Ontario threshold (often around 1.0 m in many municipalities) typically require a building permit. A good contractor will outline drainage and permit steps before you sign.
